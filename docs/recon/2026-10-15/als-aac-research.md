## 1. ALS progression and communication access by stage

### Speech
- Rate falls before intelligibility. The clinical referral marker is a speaking rate of 125 words per minute on the Sentence Intelligibility Test even if intelligibility is still above 90%; adults without disability average about 190 wpm. Nordness et al. (nearly 300 records, three AAC centres) found 88% received timely assessments when that marker was used; Ball et al. found 96% acceptance of AAC (90% immediate, 6% delayed); about 95% of the Nebraska ALS database became unable to speak before death. [S1 PR, S2 CG]
- The ALS Association's own guide for families repeats the trigger as rate at or below 125 wpm and intelligibility below 80%, and warns that insurance funding is not usually available until speech is moderately or severely impaired, that prior authorisation takes 8–12 weeks or more, and that funding is typically one device every five years. [S9 CG]
- Eshghi et al. 2022 (Scientific Reports; multi-site longitudinal cohort): speaking rate declined 1.74 wpm/month in spinal onset vs 4.16 wpm/month in bulbar onset; in bulbar onset the median time from symptom onset to <120 wpm was 23 months and to <85% intelligibility 32 months; 60% of spinal-onset participants had not reached 120 wpm after 60 months. [S3 PR]
- Judge, Ballesteros, McDermott & Bloch 2026 systematic review (28 studies, search run June 2025): need for a communication aid typically arises one to five years after diagnosis and varies strongly by site of onset, but once speech changes begin, the time to loss of functional speech is fairly consistent across types (Makkonen: ~18 months of functional communication after bulbar symptoms begin; Rong: the 150→100 wpm "intermediate phase" in fast progressors lasts ~17 months). Time from symptom onset to communication-aid use ranged from 15.5 months ("quick") to 67 months ("slow") in one cohort; eye-gaze use began between 13 and 74 months. Current guidelines were judged not to help clinicians time support; systematic monitoring of rate/intelligibility is recommended. Also: Germany reported a 93-day procurement delay and a 39% procurement failure rate, 23% because the person died first; Murphy's qualitative study saw 14 of 15 participants abandon hand-operated aids, hand access being a key reason. [S4 PR]
- Jackson et al. 2025 stage review (small open-access journal; treat as a synthesis, not primary): intelligibility typically falls below 90% within 18–24 months of symptom onset and anarthria arrives by about year three; among eye-gaze users with low daily use, "eye-gaze tiredness" was the barrier for 72.7% and oculomotor dysfunction for 18.2%; <50% of families reported receiving communication education; 78.7% of AAC users used the telephone and 84.7% video calls (citing Peters 2024). [S5 PR]
- Early AAC (tablets with communication software at ALSFRS-R speech 3–2) preserved psychological quality of life in a randomised sub-sample of bulbar-onset patients (Londral et al. 2015). [S8 PR]

### Hands, head, eyes, breathing, fatigue
- Beukelman et al. 2011: direct (hand/touch) selection is preferred as long as possible; switch scanning was reported as more fatiguing than eye tracking; head tracking can work with minimal neck control; eye tracking is often the least fatiguing access and for 53% of eye-tracking users in Ball's follow-up it was the only viable option, with 93% successful implementation. Eye-tracking users needed a mean 5 hours of instruction (2–20) and 2.27 hours of troubleshooting (0–10), mostly camera angle vs glasses glare and lighting changes. With invasive ventilation, AAC use lasts longer and eye gaze may be the only volitional movement. AAC "facilitators" were family members in 96% of cases. [S1 PR]
- The Judge review found no literature on hand-function measures that could predict when touch access will fail; physical-access milestones are "overlooked" and practice must rest on clinical judgement. [S4 PR]
- OHSU late-stage chapter (via search excerpts only): a head mouse fatigued one person quickly with frequent errors; blink-to-select is fatiguing and becomes unreliable as the disease progresses; a 2022 multimodal study found switch selection is often preferred by people who can still activate a switch because it removes the timing demand of dwell, and eye tracking plus switch was slower but more accurate than eye tracking alone. [S16 CG, S15 PR]
- Pasqualotto et al. 2015: compared with a P300 BCI, eye tracking had higher usability and lower workload for people with severe motor impairment. [S14 PR]
- Eye movements are "traditionally regarded as spared" in most ALS (oculomotor review, JAMA Neurology 2011), but late involvement occurs, and the transition to complete locked-in state includes loss of gaze fixation, which ends eye-tracker use (Tonin et al. 2020). [S12 PR, S13 PR] The ALS Association guide adds practical failure causes: prior cataract/cornea surgery may exclude eye gaze; glasses, sunlight, and medications (muscle relaxants, pain and anxiety drugs affecting pupil size) degrade tracking; fatigue and cognitive change limit use; rarely, eye control is lost entirely. [S9 CG] A vendor states that as the blink reflex weakens, corneas dry and stop reflecting infrared well, which hurts tracking especially at night. [S40 V]
- Ventilation: NIV masks interfere with speech (and saliva, mouth closure and weak cough make masks harder); after tracheostomy, voice depends on tolerating cuff deflation with a speaking valve or on ventilator setting changes; 13% of Ball's communication-aid users were on long-term ventilation. [S104 CG, S4 PR]
- Cognition: up to ~50% of people with ALS have some cognitive or behavioural change; a population-based Irish cohort found 13.8% met FTD criteria and a further 34% met cognitive-impairment criteria; language impairment is reported in roughly 23–40% of non-demented patients. Beukelman's clinic sample: 77% no impairment, 18.4% mild, 4.6% FTD, and rejection of AAC was associated with dementia. Implication (my inference): simplicity and stability matter for a meaningful minority, and some people will have word-finding trouble that pre-stored phrases help with. [S101 PR/CG, S1 PR]

### Access methods by stage (what clinicians describe)
- Early: natural speech with amplification, slower rate, quiet rooms; alphabet/topic supplementation boards; texting instead of phone calls; preparation (banking, learning the app) before it is needed. [S1, S9]
- Middle: text-based speech-generating apps on phones/tablets/laptops by touch and keyboard; the ALS Association says most people use multiple modalities and "there is no ONE system", and that the rooms with the most communication breakdowns are the bathroom and bedroom. [S10 CG]
- Late: head tracking, then switch scanning with any reliable movement (hand, foot, cheek, eyebrow, blink, sip-puff), then eye gaze; Doyle & Phillips observed increased reliance on high-tech in the middle stage and a return to low-tech approaches late; Peters found the number of communication modes rises until ALSFRS-R speech reaches 1, then falls at 0. [S9, S4]
- Very late: eye gaze with dwell; partner-assisted scanning on a letter board (partner calls rows, then letters; the person signals "yes" with a consistent movement); E-tran boards; yes/no/maybe by gaze direction. Blinks are explicitly described as unreliable in ALS (slow, uncoordinated), so gaze targets (e.g., right for yes, left for no, up for maybe) are preferred, and the agreed code must be written down and posted. [S9 CG, S10 CG] E-tran boards take anywhere from hours to over a month to learn (21% said more than a month) [S103 PR]; a high-tech eye tracker improved communication beyond an E-tran, which itself beat nothing (Caligari 2013). [S100 PR via S5]
- Voice recognition: a 2021 scoping review (11 studies, few with ALS participants) found accuracy tracks dysarthria severity; Google's Project Euphonia personalised models reached a median word error rate of 4.6% vs 31% for speaker-independent models, with the biggest gains for the most severe speakers; Voiceitt says its product is most effective for mild to moderate impairment (US$49.99/month or $600/year per its FAQ); Apple's iOS 18 "Listen for Atypical Speech" and Voice Control custom vocabulary are the on-device equivalents. Conclusion: voice is an early-stage input only, and not something Brickson should depend on. [S20 PR, S21 PR, S22 V, S55 AP]
- BCI: implanted systems in ALS report roughly 2–25 correct characters per minute in older studies and 32 wpm at 97.5% accuracy in Card et al. 2024; a 2011 survey found 72% of people with ALS would be satisfied with 15–19 letters/minute. Apple added a BCI Human Interface Device protocol to Switch Control in iOS/iPadOS 26 (announced May 2025); Synchron demonstrated an ALS participant operating an iPad through it in August 2025 (clinical-study participants only). Because the BCI appears to the system as a switch, any app that works with Switch Control works with it. [S18 PR, S5 PR, S56 AP, S73 V]

## 2. Evidence-based AAC practice for ALS

### Voice banking vs message banking
- Definitions and timing: message banking records whole phrases in the person's own voice and can only replay what was recorded (the ALS Association's example: a recorded "I need my glasses" always sounds the same); voice banking builds a synthetic voice that can say anything. Both should start as early as possible, ideally before noticeable speech change; older services needed a Windows PC, a USB noise-cancelling mic, and the ability to say ~10 words on one breath; a proxy (e.g., a relative who sounds similar) may record if speech has changed. [S9 CG] Boston Children's (Costello) reports starting message banking as early as 4 days after diagnosis (average 3 months), recommends recording in real moments rather than at a desk, and describes "double-dipping" (re-using message-bank recordings to build a synthetic voice; Costello & Fishman 2021). [S23 CG, S24 PR] Judge & Hayton reported voice-banking completion times of 7–65 weeks (older services). [S4 PR]
- Apple Personal Voice: free; iOS 17 required ~150 phrases and overnight processing; iOS 26 needs 10 phrases and under a minute on Apple-Intelligence devices (Apple newsroom; press hands-on). Languages: English (US), Spanish (Mexico), Mandarin (China mainland). It is stored encrypted on device; "Share Across Devices" keeps an end-to-end-encrypted iCloud copy so it survives device replacement (requires two-factor); apps must request access and the user can revoke it; authorised apps can speak through the speaker or during calls but cannot capture the audio; it may only be used to make a voice that sounds like the user, non-commercially; a Legacy Contact cannot access it; deletion is irreversible. Developer side: AVSpeechSynthesizer.requestPersonalVoiceAuthorization, voices flagged isPersonalVoice, and Apple says the feature should be used primarily by AAC apps. Reported quirk: if synthesis ran before authorisation in the same session, the app needs a restart (iOS 18.1 forum report). [S66 AP, S56 AP, S68 AP, S67 AP, S69 OP] Design consequence for Brickson: message-banked recordings must be ordinary audio files the app stores; they cannot be generated from Personal Voice.
- Other services (MND Association chart Oct 2025 and vendor pages): Acapela my-own-voice €99/yr or €999 one-time, works on iOS only inside partner apps (Proloquo4Text, Predictable, TD Talk); SpeakUnique £175 build, free via MND Association funding, iOS/Windows/Android; The Voice Keeper free with PRC-Saltillo products, otherwise a one-time (2023: $299) or newer monthly fee; ModelTalker free to create, ~$100 to download; ElevenLabs via Predictable and TD Talk; Team Gleason funds Acapela/ModelTalker for US residents. Tobii's TD Pilot lists Apple Personal Voice support. [S26 CG, S27 V, S28 OP, S29 CG, S30 V, S95 CG]
- Legacy-message vs synthetic difference, in practice: families value recorded "trademark" sayings, nicknames and "I love you" in the real voice; everything novel uses synthetic speech. [S9 CG]

### Rate enhancement
- Trnka et al. 2008 (17 able-bodied subjects simulating motor impairment): advanced word prediction raised communication rate 59.9% over no prediction, basic prediction 10.1%; keystroke savings 50% vs 18%; input rate falls because scanning the list costs attention, but savings dominate. Koester & Levine (1996) found the opposite under their conditions (cognitive cost "largely overwhelmed" keystroke savings). Realistic keystroke-savings ceiling ~50–60%. [S31 PR, S32 PR] Gaze typing in the wild by an ALS user averaged 8.1 ± 3.9 wpm over six months (SpeakFaster), versus ~190 wpm speech; LLM abbreviation expansion is the current research direction (Cai et al., Nature Communications 2024). [S34 PR, S33 PR] A CHI 2023 study of AAC users found AI suggestions can both help and impede: users worry about losing their own voice/agency. [S35 PR]
- Whole-phrase vs letter-by-letter: forum users describe letter-by-letter as laborious and too slow for conversation; clinicians say people with ALS who fatigue easily need many pre-stored messages, while one OT course argues pre-stored sentences are rarely used in natural conversation; there is no controlled study of category count or organisation for literate adults with ALS (consistent with docs/LESSONS.md). [S97 OP, S37 OP, S36 CG]

### Literacy-based vs symbol-based
- Apps built for adults with ALS (TD Talk, Proloquo4Text, Predictable) are text-first with phrase banks; ASHA notes semantic-syntactic displays suit adults with intact language such as people with ALS; the ALS Association calls alphabet boards the most useful low-tech aid; Predictable auto-assigns symbols to its prediction strip as an option. [S77 V, S75 V, S76 V, S36 CG, S9 CG]

### Yes/No, "maybe", and getting attention
- The ALS Association's first priority is a yes/no/maybe system (plus "I don't know", "start over", "mistake", "end of word", "space" on boards); a "maybe" is needed because a badly asked question otherwise has no answer. Blinks are unreliable in ALS; gaze directions are preferred; the mapping is chosen per person and posted on the wall. Locked-in literature likewise says up/down vs left/right mapping is per patient, not a universal convention. [S9 CG, S10 CG, S12/S13 PR]
- Alerting: buzzers; baby monitors/intercoms; wireless doorbells (~$10–20, up to ~100 ft, switch-adaptable versions exist); adapted pagers ($50+, chime or vibrate); a wired call bell with an alarm that keeps beeping until released (~$300, not covered by insurance); doorbells that alert a caregiver's phone; smart speakers for calls. [S9 CG, S10 CG]

### Pain and body location
- No validated AAC body-map board exists for ALS; ICU evidence favours observer scales (CPOT) for non-verbal patients and recommends combining communication methods. Lingraphica's free SmallTalk Pain Scale (Wong-Baker faces plus pain descriptions) is the closest shipping example on iOS. Caregivers report asking urgent yes/no questions first at night: bathroom, itch, reposition. [S102 PR, S83 V, S38 OP]

### Phone and video calls
- 78.7% of AAC users with ALS use the phone and 84.7% video calls. [S5 PR] Apple's Live Speech (iOS 17+) speaks typed text into FaceTime and phone calls with the other party hearing it; Personal Voice-authorised third-party apps may also speak "during calls" per Apple's support page; Speech Assistant AAC and Talk For Me advertise speech audible in phone/FaceTime calls; a TD Talk App Store reviewer complains the app cannot be used for calls because it lacks Slide Over. Apple's "supported assistive communication apps" integration for Live Speech is undocumented (an AAC developer's 2024 forum question has no answer). The ALS Association also recommends TTY/relay, speakerphone and amplifiers. [S65 AP, S66 AP, S81 V, S82 V, S77 OP, S110 OP, S9 CG]

### Night and bed
- One ALS caregiver's account (vendor-hosted blog, 2025): in the dark only yes/no works; blink for yes; a switch-adapted call button bumped with the head; a baby monitor; a laser pointer on glasses aimed at a ceiling board. Eye gaze is hard in the dark and with dry eyes; alsguidance.org lists bed call buttons operated by eye, voice, toe, finger or head and notes a sleeping caregiver may need very loud buzzers; a 2024 preprint reports an implanted BCI "call caregiver" signal usable at night. No source covered screen glare or bed positioning specifically. [S38 OP, S40 V, S39 OP, S42 preprint]

### Caregiver-facing needs
- Family facilitators (96%) instruct new partners, program new messages, maintain the system and call vendor support; they got just over 2 hours of training and must attend evaluations; questions families are told to ask include whether a malfunction can be repaired quickly and who will charge, turn on and program the device. [S1 PR, S9 CG] Only 46% of US respondents got SLP setup/training help; 25.7% relied on vendor representatives. [S6 PR] Caregivers with greater AAC skill reported greater rewards and more social closeness (Fried-Oken 2006). [S7 PR] Fewer than 40% of AAC users in general continue at 12 months; poor usability, learning demands, lack of expertise and physical-access difficulty are cited; ASHA cites lack of family involvement. [S43 PR, S36 CG]

### Error cost, fatigue and "communication breakdown"
- Eye-gaze tiredness is the main barrier to daily use (72.7% among low users); dwell at 500–1000 ms is said to cap speed and cause fatigue; recalibration up to 50 times a day was reported for ALS gaze typists, with muscle relaxants, antihistamines (dry eyes) and opioids (pupil size) degrading tracking (EyeO preprint). Larger paged targets cut single-switch scanning errors 35% at a 20% speed cost (Anson 2016). [S5 PR, S98 preprint, S45 PR]
- When technology fails, clinicians' answer is multimodality and a low-tech backup (alphabet board, partner-assisted scanning, posted yes/no code); families report unused devices "in the corner" when setup or calibration defeated them. [S9/S10 CG, S96 OP, S97 OP]

Dedicated devices and what they do that an iPad app cannot [S87 V, S88 V/press, S89 V, S93 GOV]:
- TD I-Series (I-13/I-16, Windows): built-in IR eye tracker that works outdoors, rear Partner Window, loud speech speakers, IR environmental control (TV etc.), TD Phone/TD Browse/TD Control for phone and full computer control, IP43 ruggedness, mounting, vendor remote support; retail cited around $11,950 for an I-13; Medicare-fundable as a "dedicated" SGD (E2510) with an SLP evaluation and physician order. TD Pilot: an eye-tracking case around an iPad, ~$10,000 at launch plus the iPad, certified to Apple's performance standards; used units $3.8–5k. PRC-Saltillo Accent 1400: 14-inch dedicated device with NuPoint head tracking and NuEye/Look eye gaze. Smartbox Grid Pad: Windows device running Grid 3 with eye gaze, environmental control and remote editing.
- Funding is the main reason dedicated devices exist: Medicare's E2510 code covers devices "only capable of speech generation"; a general-purpose computer or tablet with extra features at issue is coded A9270 (non-covered); only one SGD is covered; tablet apps are generally not reimbursed (see section 7).

What is table stakes for literate adults with ALS (every serious competitor has it): free typing with speak and Return-to-speak; word/phrase prediction; a phrase bank in categories; quick-fire interjections; history/re-say; a conversation display the partner can read; voice choice incl. Personal Voice and a banked voice; Switch Control compatibility; backup/restore; use during phone/FaceTime calls; light/dark modes.
Where a clutter-free, dwell-safe, position-stable app could win: nobody ships position stability across touch→switch→head→gaze as a design rule; nobody measures dwell safety (no opposite-in-place controls, no harmful control under the resting pointer); competitors gate speech or backup behind subscriptions/accounts (TD Snap, TD Talk, Spoken) while Brickson has no accounts; eye-gaze layouts on iPad are an afterthought everywhere (TD Talk gates gaze to Tobii hardware; Grid for iPad lists no eye gaze; Vocable only head tracking); the ALS-specific "yes/no/maybe + Emergency + Get Attention one activation away from every state" is not an explicit guarantee anywhere; no competitor advertises a no-scroll starter library or a printable low-tech board that mirrors the on-screen layout.

## 4. Apple accessibility features and what a third-party app must do

- Eye Tracking (iOS/iPadOS 18+, iPhone 12 and later, iPhone SE 3rd gen, iPad Pro 11-inch 3rd gen+/12.9-inch 5th gen+/M4, iPad 10th gen, iPad Air 3rd gen+/M2, iPad mini 6th gen, per a developer-forum transcription of Apple's footnotes; Apple's newsroom says only "iPad and iPhone"): front camera plus on-device ML, calibrates "in seconds" by looking at dots, works across built-in and third-party apps, selection via Dwell Control (AssistiveTouch); settings are Smoothing (higher = smoother, less responsive), Snap to Item (pointer jumps to the nearest selectable element), Auto-Hide (pointer appears after a steady gaze, default ~0.5 s, fades while eyes move), Dwell Control on/off; with Snap to Item off the dwell pointer appears where you look and the timer starts. Recalibration: rest eyes on the top-left corner (hot corner). iOS 26 adds the choice of a switch or dwell to select, a keyboard dwell timer and fewer steps when typing with switches. Developer requirement: none beyond standard accessible controls; it moves the AssistiveTouch pointer. Third-party commentary: eye-tracking vendors say accuracy/calibration are below dedicated hardware. [S55 AP, S56 AP, S57 AP, S58 CG, S74 OP, S85 V]
- Dwell Control (AssistiveTouch, for Eye Tracking, Head Tracking, pointer devices): dwell time adjusted with +/− buttons (Apple does not publish the range); Movement Tolerance (how far the cursor may drift while dwelling); Fallback Action (revert to Tap or Pause Dwell after an action); Hot Corners (actions when dwelling in a corner, e.g., screenshot, Control Center, Siri, scroll, shortcut); Pause Dwell. Forum users report dwell settings of 0.7–1.5 s on commercial systems. [S59 AP, S97 OP]
- AssistiveTouch: on-screen menu for gestures/buttons, virtual trackpad (iOS 18), pointer devices. [S55 AP, S59 AP]
- Switch Control: switch sources external (Bluetooth/MFi), Screen (tap/hold), Camera (left/right head movement), plus the iOS 26 BCI protocol; styles are item scanning (default), point scanning (crosshairs), manual scanning; settings include auto-scanning time, pause on first item, loops, Tap Behavior/Auto Tap interval, hold duration, ignore repeat (must be shorter than the Auto Tap interval), cursor size/colour. Developer guidance (WWDC20): an app fully accessible to VoiceOver is likely fine; grouping affects scan wait times; error tolerance matters because one accidental activation on a destructive control is serious; custom actions help. [S60 AP, S72 AP, S56 AP]
- Head Tracking: iPadOS/iOS 26 moved it to its own Accessibility item (front camera; pointer speed, tracking modes incl. "when facing screen edges"; facial expressions such as raise eyebrows/open mouth as actions with Slight/Default/Exaggerated strength; dwell timer performs Tap by default; AssistiveTouch turns on with it). [S61 AP/CG, S56 AP]
- Voice Control: "Show names"/"Show numbers" overlays; Apple's App Store Connect evaluation criteria require every interactive element to get a number and a label matching visible text; SwiftUI accessibilityInputLabels adds alternates; iOS 18 added custom vocabulary; iOS 26 syncs vocabulary across devices. Dysarthric speech limits it (section 1). [S62 AP, S63 AP, S56 AP]
- Vocal Shortcuts (iOS 18): a custom utterance or sound, spoken three times to train, triggers a Siri request, an app, an accessibility feature or a Shortcut. Could launch Brickson or trigger a Shortcut, but recognition of dysarthric utterances is untested. [S64 AP]
- Live Speech (iOS 17; iPhone XS+, iPad Air 5, iPad Pro 11 3rd/12.9 5th+): triple-click side button, type, Send; speaks into FaceTime and phone calls or the speaker; Favorite/Saved Phrases (Apple's 2024 announcement added categories); any system voice or Personal Voice. "Supported assistive communication apps" are mentioned but not documented. [S65 AP, S55 AP, S110 OP]
- Personal Voice: see section 2 (iOS 17; 10 phrases/<1 min on iOS 26 with Apple Intelligence devices; en-US, es-MX, zh-CN; iCloud E2EE sharing; app authorisation; cannot be captured). [S66 AP, S67 AP, S68 AP]
- Assistive Access (iOS 17+): a simplified system mode; in iOS/iPadOS 26 an app can provide a tailored root view through the SwiftUI AssistiveAccess scene (and accessibilityAssistiveAccessEnabled environment value); apps without one run in a reduced frame. Built-in apps include Messages, Photos, Camera, Phone, Music (per a forum post). Relevant if a family uses Assistive Access for someone with cognitive change. [S70 AP]
- Guided Access: locks the device to one app; can disable touch regions, hardware buttons, motion, software keyboard; time limits; triple-click plus passcode/Face ID to end; Crash Detection and Emergency Services are unavailable during a session. Useful for a caregiver "kiosk" setup but note the emergency-call caveat. [S71 AP]
- Full Keyboard Access: Tab/arrow focus through accessibility elements; standard controls work; WWDC21 shows custom input labels and accessibility paths for non-rectangular controls. [S72 AP]
- Accessibility Nutrition Labels (App Store, iOS 26): developers declare VoiceOver, Voice Control, Larger Text, Sufficient Contrast, Reduced Motion, etc.; voluntary now, to become required for submissions; Apple's criteria require users to complete all common tasks with the feature. Brickson's PaletteTests and Button-only rule map directly onto these. [S63 AP]
- Bottom line for Brickson: standard SwiftUI Buttons with accessibility labels, grouping that keeps scan paths short, and no control placed where Snap to Item or a hot corner would pull the pointer are the whole integration; nothing else is needed for Eye Tracking, Dwell, Switch Control (incl. BCI), Head Tracking, Voice Control or Full Keyboard Access. Live Speech's app integration and the exact Dwell time range are the two documentation gaps to hand to the apple-platform agent.

## 6. Layout research for scanning and gaze

- Anson et al. 2016 (RESNA; 25 able-bodied adults, single-switch auto-scan at 0.5 s): same display size, 6×10 single page vs four pages of 15 larger cells plus page-jump row. Single page was ~20% faster; paged had 35% fewer errors on the final trial and was the only layout whose accuracy improved with practice. Authors conclude the accuracy gain was nearly double the speed cost, so larger paged targets are generally superior, with the caveat that random symbols (no language) were used. [S45 PR]
- Findlater & McGrenere 2004 (CHI, split menus): static was faster than adaptive; adaptable matched static except when encountered first; users preferred adaptable over static but not over adaptive (secondary sources; primary numbers not retrieved). Mitchell & Shneiderman 1989 (63 subjects): people who started with dynamic (frequency-reordered) menus were significantly slower, and 81% preferred static menus. [S46 PR, S47 PR]
- Gaze target size: Namnakani et al. CHI 2025 (phone-held gaze, 25–49 cm): 4° visual angle was the "sweet spot" across screen regions and distances (≈96.6% tracking accuracy vs ≈82% at 2°); Hansen et al. ETRA 2018: effective gaze target width ≈94 px (~3°) vs ~2.5° for head/mouse; Feit et al. CHI 2017 (80 people, two trackers, two lighting conditions): accuracy/precision vary more than six-fold between users and screen regions, and target size should be derived so 95% of gaze samples land inside (a secondary summary gives ~1.9 × 2.35 cm minimum regions). At 45 cm, 4° ≈ 3.1 cm ≈ 119 pt at 163 ppi (iPad logical points, my arithmetic), which is consistent with the reference app's 110-pt tile floor and suggests quick controls at 88 pt (~3°) are near the lower bound. [S48 PR, S50 PR, S49 PR]
- Dwell time: expert gaze typists tolerated 282 ms (from an 876 ms start) with adjustable dwell (Majaranta 2009); a 2021 study recommends ~600 ms as a generic selection dwell to avoid Midas-touch errors; a 2025 meta-analysis exists on dynamic dwell systems; commercial dwell ranges 0.2–0.67 s (vendor) and 0.7–1.5 s (MND users). None of this is settable by Brickson (Apple owns dwell), but it argues for feedback that works at ~0.5–1 s and for absorbing repeats. [S51 PR, S52 PR, S53 PR, S41 V, S97 OP]
- Scanning: scan steps from the top-left corner are the efficiency metric; frequency-ordered layouts were reported 65% more efficient than alphabetic in a Scanning Wizard case series (one individual gained 321% with layout plus scan-time changes); accuracy recommendations include slower scan time, a familiar layout and manual scan initiation. This supports placing the most-used controls near the scan origin at design time, not reordering at runtime. [S54 CG/PR]
- ALS-specific: no controlled study of target size, spacing and dwell together for people with ALS on tablets between 2016 and 2026 was found; Caligari 2013 and the 2022 multimodal study are the closest. The gap docs/LESSONS.md records still stands.

## 7. Regulation and funding (short)

- US FDA: AAC hardware is regulated under 21 CFR 890.3710 "powered communication system", Class II, exempt from 510(k); the definition hinges on "intended for medical purposes". The Cures Act §3060 (2016) and FDA's 2022 software-functions guidance remove many low-risk software functions from the device definition, but neither names communication aids; the practical position (my reading) is that a phrase-speaking app marketed as a communication aid with no diagnostic/therapeutic claims is not treated as a device, and that avoiding medical claims in App Store copy matters. Tobii's I-Series manuals describe Class II DME status in the US. [S90 GOV, S91 GOV, S87 V]
- EU MDR: MDCG guidance (2019-11, revised 2025) includes a symbol-to-speech app selected by touch, head tracking or eye gaze as an example of medical device software falling in Class I under Rule 11; whether an app is a device still turns on the manufacturer's stated intended purpose. If Brickson sells in the EU with any health claim, a Class I self-declaration could be required; worth a specialist check before EU launch. [S92 GOV]
- Medicare SGD model: covered since 2001 as DME; E2510 only for devices solely capable of speech generation (PDAC-verified list), A9270 for general-purpose computers with extra features; one SGD per person; alternative input devices denied for people who can use standard input; the Steve Gleason Act 2015 covered eye-tracking accessories and removed SGDs from capped rental, made permanent by the Steve Gleason Enduring Voices Act inside the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018. Consequence: dedicated locked devices exist because of the funding rule; an App Store app on a personal iPad will be self-funded or charity-funded (Team Gleason covers SGD copays or loans devices; the MND Association loans equipment), which is exactly the segment where an app with no accounts competes. [S93 GOV, S94 GOV/CG, S95 CG, S9 CG]

## (a) Feature map by stage
TS = table stakes, D = differentiator, L = later.

Early (speech usable but slowing; hands fine; touch/typing; often still working, on the phone)
- Jobs: be understood in noise and on calls; conserve energy; prepare before need (bank voice and messages, agree yes/no code, learn the app); supplement speech.
- Features: Personal Voice preference plus a voice picker (TS); free typing with Return-to-speak (TS); message banking recorder for "trademark" phrases, with proxy recording allowed (D; this is the only window to do it); phrase editing and categories (TS); local backup/export (TS); use in phone/FaceTime calls (TS); onboarding that explains banking early and the yes/no code (D); a "practice" framing so the app is learned while speech remains (D).

Middle (speech lost; hands usable; touch, keyboard, stylus; Voice Control unreliable)
- Jobs: fast face-to-face conversation; repair misunderstandings; quick interjections; phone/video; get attention across the house; caregiver maintains content.
- Features: word/phrase prediction in a fixed strip (TS); history/re-say (TS); quick-fire interjections one tap away (TS); conversation display large enough for the partner to read, optionally flipped (TS/D); Get Attention alarm with a separate stop (TS); edit mode with confirm-and-Keep (TS); backup/restore (TS); light/dark/high-contrast (TS); printable board that mirrors the on-screen layout for the bathroom/bedroom/ER (D).

Late (hands weak; head tracking or switch scanning; maybe NIV)
- Jobs: minimum activations per message; no scrolling; stable positions so the person does not relearn; call for help; answer yes/no/maybe fast; tolerate fatigue.
- Features: stage profiles that scale targets and spacing without moving anything (D, and the core promise); scan-friendly grouping with Emergency/Get Attention/Yes/No near the scan origin (TS); dwell-safe placement, no opposite-in-place controls (D); loud alarm from the device (TS); caregiver-set timeouts that reset rather than close (D); call via the system (Live Speech/phone) rather than in-app network code (L).

Very late (eyes only, or BCI through Switch Control; bed-bound; night)
- Jobs: yes/no/maybe/"I don't know"/"start over"; pain and body location; position changes; call for help in the dark; keep the partner informed when the tracker fails.
- Features: gaze profile with ≥4° targets and generous gaps (TS for this stage); yes/no/maybe plus "I don't know" and "start over" tiles (TS); body-location/pain/position board (D); absorbing repeated dwell on the phrase being spoken (TS); printable E-tran/letter-board export of the same layout for partner-assisted scanning (D); BCI works automatically because the app is standard-button only (TS by construction); night mode (dark, low glare, large) (D).

## (b) The ten capabilities Brickson most needs, ranked
1. Typing with speak (Return speaks; a large keyboard above the worst-case software keyboard). Evidence: every ALS-focused competitor is text-first; the ALS Association's purchase checklist asks whether a device can "write, store and say anything"; Peters found >90% of severely impaired people use SGDs; letter-by-letter is slow, so prediction follows at #7. [S9, S6, S75–77]
2. Edit mode for the person and the family facilitator (add, edit, reorder within fixed slots, categories, confirm-and-Keep). Evidence: facilitators are family in 96% of cases and their job is programming new messages; families rank staying connected as the top AAC function; abandonment follows when customisation is hard. [S1, S7, S43]
3. Get Attention: a loud alarm, start-only control, stop elsewhere, usable through a baby monitor. Evidence: call systems are the first thing clinicians set up; the bedroom and bathroom are where breakdowns happen; commercial call-bell alarms cost ~$300 and are not covered. [S9, S10, S38, S39]
4. Backup, export and restore without an account (Files/iCloud Drive/AirDrop, human-readable). Evidence: TD Talk reviewers resent needing an account to back up their own words; AssistiveWare tells users to export backups regularly; procurement failures and device deaths leave families without words; invariant 7 means a restore must never delete. [S77, S75, S4]
5. Stage profiles (touch, switch, head, gaze) that change size and spacing only. Evidence: Beukelman says recommended technology must have adjustable access options to carry a person from hand access to scanning, head or eye tracking; Murphy's abandonment was driven by failing hand access; the Judge review found no predictor of when touch fails, so the switch must be easy to make at home. [S1, S4]
6. Message banking (own-voice recordings on tiles) and a voice picker (Personal Voice, system voices, banked voice). Evidence: Costello, the ALS Association and RCSLT all say bank early; Personal Voice cannot be captured to a file, so recordings are a separate feature; TD Talk, Predictable and Grid all support banked messages. [S9, S23, S24, S66, S77, S76, S78]
7. Prediction and history/re-say in a fixed strip. Evidence: +10–60% communication rate with prediction; gaze typists average ~8 wpm; every competitor has both; the conflict with fixed positions is resolvable (see e). [S31, S34, S75]
8. Yes/No/Maybe plus "I don't know"/"start over", with a printable partner-assisted board that mirrors the layout. Evidence: the ALS Association calls a yes/no/maybe system critical to medical management and life-saving in ERs and shelters; late-stage users return to low-tech; blinks are unreliable so the app must not assume a gesture. [S9, S10, S4]
9. Phone and FaceTime use (speech audible in calls; a conversation view the partner can read). Evidence: 78.7% phone and 84.7% video use; Live Speech sets the user expectation; TD Talk is criticised for lacking it; Apple permits Personal Voice apps to speak during calls. [S5, S65, S66, S77]
10. Onboarding and setup help for the family (first-run that sets the profile, voice, yes/no code, alarm test, backup; Accessibility Nutrition Labels; Voice Control labels; a Guided Access/Assistive Access note). Evidence: only 46% got SLP setup help and <50% of families got communication education; forum stories of devices abandoned after failed setup; Apple's labels will become mandatory. [S6, S5, S96, S63]
Also considered: night mode (D), pain/body board (D), Siri/App Intents launch (L), Vocal Shortcuts launch (L).

## (c) Things to avoid, with evidence
- Adaptive or frequency-based reordering: 81% preferred static menus and first-exposure users were slower (Mitchell & Shneiderman); static beat adaptive (Findlater & McGrenere); constantly changing layouts reduce satisfaction. [S47, S46]
- Subscriptions or accounts that gate speech or backup: the single most bitter App Store complaint class (TD Snap, TD Talk, Spoken). [S77, S79]
- Confirm-to-speak, load-then-speak, or in-place start/stop toggles: dwell users rest on what they read; Apple's own Switch Control guidance warns one accidental activation on a destructive control is serious. [S72, S98]
- Small or crowded gaze targets and scrolling: 2° targets drop to ~82% accuracy; paged larger targets cut errors 35%; scrolling costs several activations under dwell. [S48, S45]
- Custom gaze, blink or head code: Apple's layers already work across apps and blink-to-select is fatiguing and unreliable in ALS. [S55, S16, S9]
- Hard-coding a yes/no gesture or direction: clinicians set it per person. [S9, S12]
- Relying on Voice Control or voice recognition for the person: accuracy tracks dysarthria severity. [S20]
- Timers, decoy buttons or "are you sure" dialogs as safety: fatigue and dwell make them traps; use placement (already an AGENTS.md rule, now evidence-backed by the Switch Control guidance and dwell research). [S72, S52]
- AI-generated replies or ChatGPT inside the app: AAC users report loss of agency and authenticity with AI text; it also violates invariant 9. [S35]
- Clutter (translation, chat assistants, symbol strips) in the person's path: competitors' feature creep is exactly what forum users call too slow and hard to navigate. [S76, S79, S97]
- Head tracking or any alert on by default: Vocable reviewers asked for the opposite. [S80]
- Medical claims in store copy (changes regulatory status in the US/EU). [S90–S92]

## (d) Open questions only a person living with ALS, a caregiver or an SLP can answer
1. When you are tired, do you want fewer, bigger phrases on the Home screen or more phrases behind one extra activation? What is the most you would accept per screen?
2. Which five phrases do you use most in a day, and which five at night? Which should sit nearest the scan start / least effortful gaze spot?
3. Which yes/no/maybe signals do you and your family use, and what else do you need on that board ("I don't know", "start over", "something's wrong")?
4. For Get Attention: what sound, how loud, how long, and who should be able to stop it? Is a sound that keeps going until a caregiver stops it reassuring or frightening?
5. Do you want a body-location/pain board, and in what words (your words, not ours)? What about position changes (turn me, lift my head, suction, ventilator)?
6. How did you choose between banked recordings and a synthetic voice? Would you want a tile to play your recording when one exists and fall back to the synthetic voice otherwise, or keep them visibly separate?
7. Who edits the phrases, how often, and on whose device? Would a printed copy of the screen help in the bathroom, car, ER?
8. When the eye tracker fails (calibration, light, dry eyes, meds), what do you fall back to, and how long before you give up on the device for the day?
9. On calls: do you use the phone/FaceTime with a speech device, and what breaks (hearing yourself, the other person's patience, the partner typing for you)?
10. How much warning do you want when the app is about to change size/spacing for a new profile, and should the family or the person make that switch?
11. For SLPs: which ALSFRS-R or hand/neck milestones do you use to time the move from touch to head/switch to gaze, and what dwell times do your clients settle on?
12. For SLPs: what minimum phrase set should a starter library contain for a literate adult, and what must it never contain?

## (e) Where common features conflict with Brickson's hard rules
- Word/phrase prediction vs "position is never a function of usage" (invariant 8): the conflict is partial. The evidence against adaptive layouts is about menus whose item positions move; prediction strips in every competitor are a fixed region whose contents change, which AGENTS.md already allows for a recents zone. Resolution used by Proloquo4Text, TD Talk, Grid and Predictable: fixed strip, fixed slot count, contents vary; Brickson can adopt the same with the strip placed away from Emergency/Yes/No and with no speaking control nearest a predicted word. The residual risk (dwell landing on a changed suggestion) is real and should be scored like any mode switch. [S31, S47, S75–78]
- History/recents vs fixed positions: same resolution (fixed zone, changing contents) and the same dwell-safety scoring; Vocable and Proloquo4Text both ship a Recents/History list. [S80, S75]
- Caregiver alerts vs no network code (invariant 9): real conflict for remote alerts. Competitors solve it with hardware (TD Phone on Windows devices, Bluetooth switch-adapted doorbells, pagers) or voice assistants. Brickson's in-scope answer is a loud local alarm heard through a baby monitor (what clinicians recommend anyway); a later, owner-decided path is handing off to the system through App Intents/Shortcuts so the Shortcuts app sends the message (no network code in Brickson), subject to the privacy-policy rule that App Shortcut phrases never contain phrase words. [S10, S38, S87]
- Message banking vs "never put words about a person's condition in their mouth" (invariant 7): no conflict; the recordings are the person's own words. Proxy banking (a relative records) is a design question for the owner: clinicians endorse it, the invariant does not forbid it, but tiles must show whose voice plays.
- Scan-efficiency layouts (frequency-ordered) vs fixed positions: no conflict if frequency informs the design-time layout and user-chosen slot assignments, never runtime reordering. [S54]
- Snap to Item and hot corners vs dwell safety (invariant 10): Apple's pointer will snap to the nearest control and the top-left corner recalibrates; that is why nothing harmful may be nearest to text or empty space, and why corners stay clear, as AGENTS.md already says. [S57, S58]
- Cloud sync/accounts vs backup: competitors use accounts (TD Talk) or third-party drives (Proloquo4Text); a local export to Files/iCloud Drive meets the need without accounts or network code in the app, and Personal Voice's own iCloud sharing is handled by the system. [S77, S75, S66]
- Subscriptions: no conflict, but "Price not decided" must hold in all copy; no price is decided, and the subscription complaints above (sections 3 and 5) are evidence for the owner's decision.
